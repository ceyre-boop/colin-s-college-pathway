# Adam Ferrari Health Science Scholarship

Eleven minutes. That's how long it took me to outline one tumor on one slice of one MRI, and when I finished there were forty more slices in the stack.

I was sixteen, at my uncle's radiation oncology clinic in North Dakota, and he'd let me sit down at the planning workstation and trace the boundary of a real person's cancer with a mouse. Click, drag, click, one vertex at a time, all the way around a gray smudge that didn't have edges so much as it had opinions. Where the tumor stopped and healthy tissue started was a judgment call. I made it about three hundred times that afternoon.

Here's the part I couldn't stop chewing on, and honestly still can't. Everything else in that building was extraordinary. The scanners, the linear accelerator, the physics of aiming radiation through a skull without cooking the parts you need — all of it decades ahead of what I expected. And then the step that decides where the beam actually goes was a teenager with a mouse, because the software wasn't good enough and somebody had to do it.

Nobody writes about that step. It isn't the discovery, it isn't the cure, it's the boring middle where a tired person traces lines for an hour. It's also, as far as I can tell, where a real amount of outcome quality quietly lives.

I've spent the two years since then getting good at exactly that kind of unglamorous middle. I'm the founding AI engineer at TABOOST, the largest social media agency in the country — they cleared five million in profit last year — and what I built there reads and routes over two hundred emails a day across sixteen inboxes. It is not interesting work to describe at a party. It's classification under noise, on live data, where being wrong costs somebody something real, and it has to be right on a Tuesday when nobody is watching.

Markets and inboxes and tumors are not the same problem, but they have the same skeleton: messy input, a decision with consequences, and no credit for getting it right. I like that shape. I appear to be built for it.

So I'm doing the biology degree, and the computational work is already running, and the thing at the end of it is software that proposes the boundary so an oncologist verifies instead of traces.

Somewhere there's a version of that afternoon where the smudge already has a line around it when the doctor sits down, and he spends his eleven minutes on the patient instead. That's the one I'm building toward.
