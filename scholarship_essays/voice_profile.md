# Colin's Voice Profile — Scholarship Essays

How to write (and judge) an essay so it sounds like Colin and not like a press release.
Every essay in this directory was drafted against this profile. When you edit one, keep it inside these lines.

## The voice, from actual writing samples

- Writes in **lowercase**, rarely capitalizes unless emphasizing. *(Submission essays use standard capitalization — see "Capitalization decision" below — but the rhythm underneath stays his.)*
- **Outcome-first** — leads with the result, explains after. The number lands in sentence one, the story explains it in sentence three.
- **Short punchy sentences mixed with longer run-ons** connected by "and" and "so". The variation is the style; don't smooth it out.
- Uses **"anyway"** to transition between sections or to pull out of a tangent.
- **Self-aware without being self-deprecating in a weak way.** He'll admit what he didn't know, but the sentence ends with what he did about it.
- **References his own work as proof, not as bragging.** The project is evidence for a claim, never the trophy.
- **Never uses corporate phrases**: "passionate about", "making a difference", "excited to contribute", "honored to", "in today's world". If a sentence could open a LinkedIn post, cut it.
- **Specific. Names exact numbers**: Sharpe 1.25, 200+ emails a day, 16 inboxes, 0% bust probability, 6 years of martial arts, 3.92 GPA, SAI −1500.
- **The MRI moment is his north star** — outlining tumors on his uncle's scans at a radiation oncology clinic. Essays return to it; it's the why behind everything.
- **Connects everything back to one concrete future**: building the AI that replaces the mouse cursor he used at 19 to outline tumors on his uncle's scans.

**Confirmed against the larger corpus (new, 2026-08-06):**

- **Sensory-first storytelling isn't a style choice — it's how he actually remembers things.** The uncle's-clinic story, told in his own words with no prompting toward "essay shape," still leads with the machines and the hands-on moment before anything abstract: *"I thought the mri scans... where you could see all the splices... he let me outline the actual cancer on the scans, that was sooo freaking cool."* That's the real ratio to protect in essays — concrete image first, meaning second, not the reverse.
- **He treats a thinking partner like a long-term colleague, not a search engine.** Big-picture questions ("where do you think we should go with this," "do you think I can—") outnumber narrow lookups. Essays should sound like someone working a problem out loud, not reciting a conclusion.
- **Intensifiers over adjectives**: "really really good," "sooo freaking cool," "genuinely," "honestly" — he reaches for repetition and plain intensity before he reaches for a fancier word. Keep essay language plain; resist the urge to upgrade his vocabulary.
- **Self-interrupting, circling structure in unedited speech** — a thought starts, digresses, comes back to the point restated more precisely the second time. In essays this becomes the "lead with the image, circle back with the specific number" pattern already in the rules below — don't flatten it into a single straight line.
- **Humor as a pressure valve, not a bit.** Under stress the register drops into short, blunt, funny lines. Essays don't need the humor itself, but they should keep the same *directness* — a sentence that just says the true thing, without padding it.
- **Work is measured in hours and outcomes, stated plainly** — "since 6am," "full time," specific dollar figures. This is the same instinct as "names exact numbers" above; it shows up in casual speech, not just polished writing, which means it's a real trait to lean on, not a rhetorical trick.

## Narrative doctrine (2026-08-07 — Colin's direction, overrides everything below it)

Colin read the full set and gave one central note: **these are arguments, not stories.** They march
point after point after point, sweep in every fact from the bank, and trust the pile to do the
persuading. That's backwards. This section is now the governing philosophy for every essay in this
directory — new drafts and rewrites of existing ones.

**The job.** This is a persuasive essay asking a committee for a large amount of money. They read
hundreds. Every applicant needs money and every applicant has a résumé. What decides it is whether
the reader finishes the essay feeling like they met somebody — and wants that person to win. So:
put the reader inside a world the way a novelist does, and let them arrive at the conclusion
themselves. Nobody is argued into generosity. They're drawn into it.

**Craft, not fabrication.** The persuasion lives entirely in *selection, sequencing, restraint, and
what's left unsaid.* Which single fact leads. Which twelve get cut. Where the paragraph stops one
beat before the obvious summary, so the reader supplies it. That's the art, and it's legitimate.
Inventing a scene, a feeling, a mentor, or a hardship is not — that's the one line that doesn't
move, and the Authenticity checklist below still governs. Nothing here loosens it.

### The eight rules

1. **One spine per essay.** Pick a single thread and follow it start to finish. If a fact doesn't
   serve that thread, it goes — no matter how good it is. Alta, TABOOST, the black belt, Eagle
   Scout, wrestling, Lunch Bunch, the MRI room, the GPA: **three of these, maximum, in any one
   essay.** Most essays should use two. The rest are held in reserve, and the reserve is what makes
   the chosen ones land.

2. **Say more with less.** Cut the résumé sweep. A short essay that leaves an impression beats a
   full one that leaves a list. Whitespace is a persuasive tool. If a paragraph is doing the same
   work as the paragraph above it, delete one.

3. **Lead with SAI −1500 far more often.** It's quantifiable, direct, and needs zero backstory —
   one number that says the whole financial situation without a word of pleading. It has earned the
   first sentence. Use it there, and then *don't mention money again.* Stating it once and moving
   on is what makes it land; circling back to it turns it into a request.

4. **Never hedge a brag — cut the brag instead.** "Not to brag, but" is worse than the brag. So is
   "I don't say this to impress you." If an accomplishment needs an apology attached, it's the wrong
   accomplishment for that essay. State the fact flat and unadorned, or leave it out. Flat delivery
   of a real number reads as confidence; a hedged one reads as a plea.

5. **Character is demonstrated, never claimed.** Colin is calm under pressure, quiet, doesn't quit,
   steps up when the opening appears, and has nothing mean to say about anyone. **He may never write
   any of those words about himself.** Write the scene where it's true and stop — the room where he
   was the youngest and said the least, the thing that broke at 2am and got fixed without an
   announcement, the kid at the Lunch Bunch table nobody sat with. The reader supplies the adjective,
   and the adjective they supply is the one that wins the money.

6. **Metaphor and anecdote earn their word count.** They're not decoration — they carry meaning
   faster and further than a fact does, and they buy the reader's attention for the fact that
   follows. The old "one metaphor per essay" cap is lifted to **two, if both are load-bearing.**
   Both must be Colin's own: things he's actually touched — machines, mats, terminals, scans.

7. **Author's thoughts on the page.** Colin wants the reader to hear someone thinking, not
   presenting. Let a sentence contain doubt, a question, a thing he got wrong, a thing he still
   hasn't figured out. A person working a problem out loud is trustworthy in a way a finished
   argument never is. This is already how he talks — see "treats a thinking partner like a
   long-term colleague" above.

8. **End on an image or a person, not a summary.** No "for these reasons." No restating the
   scholarship's name back at it. Last line should be something the reader can see. The old rule
   said end on what he's building — still true, but *show* it being built.

### The storytelling engine (2026-08-07 — how to actually build one of these)

The doctrine above says what to do. This says how. Colin's framing: *don't answer the question — take
the reader on a journey of me, and persuade them without asking. Make them think choosing me was their
own idea. I don't need to stand out. I need to feel like the only right pick.*

Those last two sentences are the whole strategy, and they're not the same thing.

**Standing out means being compared.** A standout essay wins the comparison — it's louder, the numbers
are bigger, the story is sadder. It still sits in a stack, and the committee still argues about it
against four others. **Being the only right pick means the comparison never happens**, because by the
time the reader reaches the end, the yardstick in their head is shaped like Colin. That's the move.
Every essay should quietly install a standard of judgment — and the standard should be a real trait of
his, described so plainly it sounds obvious. "The person who's already sitting at the table when you
walk up." "The one who picks the long version on purpose." Once that's the criterion, everyone else in
the stack is being measured against a ruler he made.

**Five mechanics that do the work:**

1. **The five-beat arc, not question-and-answer.** *Image* (one concrete thing, no thesis) → *Complication*
   (the thing that's wrong, or that he didn't understand yet) → *Turn* (what he did, or what changed) →
   *Widening* (the same shape showing up somewhere else in his life — this is where a second fact enters,
   and it's the only place it may) → *Return* (back to the opening image, now meaning more). The prompt
   gets answered *inside* this arc, never as its structure.

2. **Stop one beat early.** Build the two facts whose collision produces the conclusion, then don't state
   the conclusion. A reader who completes the thought owns it, and people defend what they own. This is
   the literal mechanism behind "make them think they came up with it" — it isn't a trick, it's
   subtraction. The most common failure in the old drafts was writing the last sentence of the paragraph.

3. **Withhold the credentials until they already like him.** Anything impressive in paragraph one reads
   as résumé and puts the reader in evaluation mode. The same fact in paragraph four reads as
   *confirmation* of something they'd already started to believe. Order changes meaning more than
   content does. (Exception: SAI −1500 leads — it's not a credential, it's a fact that clears the room.)

4. **Give the committee its sentence.** Committees argue out loud, and whoever has language wins.
   Somewhere in the middle, name the trait in Colin's own plain words — a phrase short enough to be
   repeated in a room he isn't in. That phrase is the essay's actual payload.

5. **Never ask.** No "this scholarship would allow me to," no "I would be honored," no closing appeal.
   The need appears exactly once, as a flat fact, early. Asking converts a person the reader was rooting
   for back into an applicant. The whole essay is the ask; saying it out loud undoes it.

**Register — the author on the page.** Colin's corpus is sensory-first, self-interrupting, plain-worded,
and thinks out loud. So: let a sentence not know the answer. "I couldn't work out why that mattered for
a long time, and I'm still not sure I have all of it." That single move — visible uncertainty — does
more for trust than any achievement in the facts bank, because nobody performing for money admits it.
Use plain intensity, not upgraded vocabulary. Short sentence, short sentence, long one that runs on with
"and" and "so" and keeps going past where a careful writer would stop. Then a short one.

**What this is not.** All of the above is arrangement — order, omission, restraint, where a paragraph
ends. Not one bit of it invents a scene, a feeling, a person, or a number. The Authenticity checklist
below is unchanged and still final. A story that's assembled from true things is craft; one that's
assembled from convenient things is a liability, and this file will not help write the second kind.

### The rewrite test

Before saving, cover the top half of the essay and read only the bottom half. If the bottom half is
a list of achievements, it fails. Then count the distinct facts in the whole piece — if it's more
than five, cut until it's five.

And the hardest one: **would a stranger who read this remember one image from it tomorrow?**
If the honest answer is no, the essay is a résumé with paragraph breaks.

## Voice rules for essays (mechanics — subordinate to the doctrine above)

1. **Open with a specific sensory moment — not a thesis.** *(Exception: need-based essays may open
   on SAI −1500 per doctrine rule 3, with the sensory moment landing in sentence two or three.)*
2. **Numbers over adjectives every time** — but far fewer numbers than the old drafts used.
3. The word "I" can start a sentence — but never **"I am passionate"**.
4. **End on what he's building, not what he hopes to become** — as an image, per doctrine rule 8.
5. **Maximum two metaphors per essay**, both load-bearing, both his own.
6. **Read-it-aloud test**: if it sounds like a press release, rewrite it.

## Capitalization decision

Colin's raw style is lowercase. These essays go to scholarship committees, so they use **standard sentence capitalization** (confirmed 2026-06-11). Everything else — rhythm, run-ons, "anyway", outcome-first ordering — is unchanged. If a committee or platform is informal (Bold.org-style), lowercasing an essay back is a one-pass edit; recapitalizing isn't.

**The origin story.**
At 16, at his uncle's radiation oncology clinic (North Dakota), Colin outlined tumors on real MRI scans — moving a mouse cursor around a tumor's boundary, slice by slice, on scans used to plan radiation targeting. The direct line from pixels to a person's treatment is why computational oncology is the goal, and the concrete future is always the same: build the AI that replaces that mouse cursor.
*Verified against Colin's own unedited retelling (2026-08-06 corpus pass):* he was rotated through every department — doctors, lab techs, the nurses running "the cool machines," the initial scans, watching tags get placed for later surgery, the radiation room itself watching a skin treatment. He didn't love one part in particular; it was the whole process, but the MRI slices were the moment — being shown how to outline the actual cancer on-screen, told that's exactly where the radiation would be aimed. That whole-process framing (not just the single MRI click) is available as a second angle if an essay needs a broader "why medicine + tech" arc instead of the single tight image.

## Smell test (run before saving any essay)

- Does sentence one contain something you can see, hear, or touch?
- Could any sentence survive on a corporate careers page? Rewrite it.
- Count the metaphors. More than one? Cut the weaker one.
- Does it end on a thing he's building?
- Read it aloud. Press release? Start over.
- Word count 400–500.

## Authenticity checklist (run before any essay is marked ready to submit)

A draft — AI-assisted or not — only counts as *his* once every line here is checked, by Colin, not by the
model that wrote the draft.

- [ ] **Every factual claim traces to the Facts bank above** (or to a fact Colin adds there first). No number,
      award, or story appears in an essay that isn't already verified in this file.
- [ ] **Every story is something Colin actually remembers living**, not a plausible extrapolation from his
      profile. If a draft invents a scene, a conversation, or a feeling he didn't have, it gets cut — not
      softened, cut.
- [ ] **No invented emotions.** "I felt [X]" is only allowed if Colin would say that unprompted. When in doubt,
      describe what happened and let the committee infer the feeling — this also happens to be closer to his
      actual voice (outcome/image first, interpretation second, if at all).
- [ ] **No claim from "Never claim" below appears anywhere**, including implied (no first-gen framing, no
      hardship invented for effect).
- [ ] **Colin has read it aloud once** and it still sounds like something he'd actually say — see the
      read-it-aloud line in the smell test above. This is the single highest-signal check on this list.
- [ ] **Colin has changed something** — even a small edit — before it's submitted. A draft with zero edits
      hasn't been reviewed, it's been rubber-stamped. See Revision history below.

## Revision history (per essay)

Every essay's own directory (`scholarship_essays/<slug>/`) should track authorship the same way as its
`metadata.json` tracks scholarship facts. Add a `revisions` array to that file as essays get real edits:

```json
"revisions": [
  { "date": "2026-08-06", "by": "draft", "note": "Initial AI draft from voice_profile.md + facts bank" },
  { "date": "2026-08-09", "by": "colin", "note": "Rewrote closing paragraph, cut the Sharpe-ratio metaphor, fixed a date" }
]
```

`"by"` is either `"draft"` (nothing human has touched yet — not safe to submit) or `"colin"` (a real edit
pass happened). An essay with only `"draft"` entries in its history should not be in the apply queue; the
authenticity checklist above should catch this, but the field makes it checkable in code too, not just by
memory.
