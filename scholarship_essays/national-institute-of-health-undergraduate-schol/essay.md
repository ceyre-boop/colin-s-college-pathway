# NIH Undergraduate Scholarship Program

By the third slice I had stopped thinking about the software.

The scan was already up when I sat down — a brain in grayscale, sliced thin, forty-some images deep. I was sixteen, at my uncle's radiation oncology clinic in North Dakota, and the task he'd given me was to drag the cursor around the tumor's edge on each slice so the treatment plan would know where to aim. The first slice took eleven minutes. Slices one and two, I was thinking about the interface, the way you do.

Then somewhere in the third one it stopped being a shape. There was a person attached to it, with a Thursday appointment, and I was deciding by eye where their cancer ended.

I have not really stopped thinking about that since, and the specific thing I can't let go of isn't the emotion of it. It's that this is the normal workflow. Not a shortcut, not an under-resourced clinic — this is how radiation targeting is done in most of the country. The imaging is extraordinary and the physics is extraordinary and the last judgment call is a tired human with a mouse, because the segmentation software isn't good enough to be trusted and somebody has to be the fallback.

That's a research problem sitting in the open, and it's the one I want.

I can already do the computational half at production grade. I'm the founding AI engineer at TABOOST — the largest social media agency in the United States, five million in profit last year — where the system I built reads and routes over two hundred emails a day across sixteen inboxes. At eighteen I built Alta Investments alone, a quantitative trading system that trades live on a Sharpe of 1.25 with probability of ruin held at zero. Noisy input, high stakes, a decision that has to be defensible: I've built that loop and kept it running.

What I don't have yet is the biology, which is why the degree isn't optional. You cannot build a model that decides where tissue changes without understanding what is changing. Cellular and molecular first, then the imaging work, and I'd rather learn it in a lab than infer it from papers.

Research careers are long and I'm aware that most of this will not work. That's fine — I've spent six years on things before, and the useful skill was never talent, it was staying past the point where it stopped being fun.

I'd like the thing I stay on to be that room.

Forty slices, one line at a time, until it doesn't have to be done that way.
