# Amazon Future Engineer Scholarship

Before Alta Investments ever placed a trade, I spent weeks on the part of it that makes no money: the risk model.

I was eighteen. I'd built a quantitative trading system by myself — no co-founder, no course, no adult in the loop — and the obvious thing to do next was turn it on and see what happened. Instead I sat there writing the code that decides when the system is not allowed to act. Position limits. Drawdown ceilings. The arithmetic that answers "what sequence of bad days ends this account," which I ground down to zero probability of ruin before I let it touch real capital.

That's a strange way to spend your enthusiasm. But I'd already learned where the interesting failure lives, and it wasn't in the strategy.

Two years earlier I was sixteen, sitting at a treatment planning workstation in my uncle's radiation oncology clinic, outlining a tumor on an MRI with a mouse. Eleven minutes a slice, forty more slices waiting. The scanner was miraculous. The accelerator that would fire down the line I drew was miraculous. The line itself was drawn by a kid who'd been shown how twenty minutes earlier, because the segmentation software wasn't good enough to be trusted and a human had to be the fallback.

That's when I understood what engineering actually is. It isn't the impressive component. It's knowing which part of your system is load-bearing and being honest about which part is a person quietly compensating for something you didn't build well enough.

Alta runs today with a Sharpe ratio of 1.25 and that ruin probability still pinned at zero, on live data, every trading day. The strategy is the part people ask about. The brake is the part I'm proud of.

So the degree is biology, and the thing at the end of it is the segmentation problem — software that proposes the tumor boundary so the oncologist verifies instead of traces. It's genuinely hard, and not in the "nobody's tried" way. It's hard because if the model is confidently wrong about where a tumor ends, the failure isn't a bad quarter. It's a dose delivered into tissue somebody needed.

Which means the first real thing I build there won't be the model. It'll be whatever tells the model to shut up and hand the decision back to a human.

I already know how to write that part. I wrote it at eighteen for money, which matters a great deal less.
