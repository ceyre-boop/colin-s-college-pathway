# NASE Future Entrepreneur Award

Everyone I know who wants to start a company can describe the upside of their idea. I've never met one who could describe the downside in the same amount of detail.

That's the whole job, as far as I can tell. I found out at eighteen, the morning Alta Investments went live and I sat watching a terminal move real money on decisions my code was making, with nobody above me to escalate to. I had thought of that as freedom. Around nine-thirty it reorganized itself into something else.

The reason I could sit there at all is that I'd spent the weeks before launch on the part of the business that generates nothing: the risk model. Position limits, drawdown ceilings, and the arithmetic that answers "what sequence of bad days ends this account," which I ground to zero probability of ruin before it touched a dollar. The strategy is the part people ask about. It runs on a Sharpe of 1.25. The brake is the part that let me sleep.

Building the brake first is a strange instinct for a nineteen-year-old and I think it came from watching a business that was already at scale. I'm the founding AI engineer at TABOOST — the largest social media agency in the United States, five million in profit last year. What I built there reads and routes over two hundred emails a day across sixteen inboxes. At that size you learn quickly that a company isn't the idea, it's the thousand unglamorous systems that keep the idea from costing somebody their client on a Tuesday. Nobody founds a company for the systems. Every company that lasts is made of them.

So I know what I'm actually good at, and it isn't having ideas. It's owning the failure case of somebody else's afternoon.

The business I'm building toward is medical. When I was sixteen my uncle let me sit at the planning workstation in his radiation oncology clinic and outline a tumor on an MRI — cursor, gray smudge, eleven minutes for one slice, forty more waiting, and a machine that would aim down my line on Thursday. That step still gets done by hand in most of the country. It is a large, boring, expensive bottleneck sitting directly upstream of whether people's cancer treatment is accurate.

Alta funds the education. TABOOST is where I learned what scale actually requires. The biology degree supplies the part I don't have yet.

And the company at the end of it has exactly one job: put the line on the scan before the doctor sits down.
